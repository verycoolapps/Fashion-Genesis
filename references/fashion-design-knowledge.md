# Fashion Design Knowledge Base

Reference module for Fashion Genesis X. Covers silhouette taxonomy, construction fundamentals, category conventions, color theory, proportion principles, and the working vocabulary used by patternmakers, designers, and merchandisers.

Status convention used throughout: **[VERIFIED]** = established industry practice, **[EXPERIMENTAL]** = demonstrated in research/labs/small runs, **[SPECULATIVE]** = conceptual, unproven at scale.

---

## 1. Silhouette Taxonomy

A silhouette is the two-dimensional outline a garment produces on the body. It is the first thing a customer perceives and the primary decision made before any detail. Taxonomy below is the working vocabulary.

### 1.1 Classic Silhouette Families

| Family | Definition | Body interaction | Canonical references |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Column / Sheath** | Narrow, vertical, follows body line | Skin-close or close | Bias-cut gowns, bodycon dresses, pencil skirts |
| **A-line** | Fitted at shoulder/bust, flares linearly to hem | Skims hips, adds volume below waist | Dior "New Look" 1947, trapeze dresses |
| **Shift** | Boxy, hangs from shoulder, no waist shaping | Hangs free of torso | 1960s Courrèges, little black dress |
| **Oversized** | Deliberately larger than body in all axes | Pools around body, creates void | Margiela "deconstructed" jackets, Fear of God Essentials |
| **Tent / Empire** | Seams gathered under bust, volume flows from there | Shoulders-to-bust fitted, rest voluminous | Empire-waist Regency gowns, muumuu-derived resort wear |
| **Fit-and-flare** | Fitted bodice, defined waist, flared skirt | Waist emphasized, hip volume | 1950s swing skirts, prom dresses |
| **Ball gown** | Structured bodice + very full skirt | Extreme lower-body volume | Couture evening, debutante |
| **Mermaid / Trumpet** | Fitted through thigh, flares at or below knee | Constrains legs until flare | Red-carpet gowns |
| **Harem / Sarouel** | Crotch dropped low, wide tapered legs | Volume at hip/crotch | Middle Eastern tailoring, Virgil Abloh |
| **Culotte / Wide-leg** | Full-width leg, no taper | Leg volume, elongates lower body | 1970s wide-leg trousers, SS18 runways |

### 1.2 Structural/Architectural Silhouettes

These break the above families because their form comes from construction technique, not draping:

- **Origami fold** — hard creases, geometric facets. **[VERIFIED]** Issey Miyake's 125 lines, Comme des Garçons cardboard constructions.
- **Pleated columns** — knife pleats or accordion pleats carry structure without interfacing. **[VERIFIED]** Issey Miyake Pleats Please (polyester heat-set pleats).
- **Cocoon / Cocoon coat** — rounded, padded or filled volume enclosing the body. **[VERIFIED]** 1980s power dressing, contemporary outerwear.
- **Deconstructed** — exposed seams, raw edges, inside-out construction. **[VERIFIED]** Martin Margiela, Rick Owens. The "unfinished" look is a deliberate design statement, not poor execution.
- **Architectural padding** — sculpted foam, boning, or molded components create non-fabric form. **[VERIFIED]** Comme des Garçons 1997 "lumps and bumps" collection; **[EXPERIMENTAL]** 3D-printed structural panels (e.g., Danit Peleg's 3D-printed gown).
- **Modular / Transformable** — garment comprises separable units. **[EXPERIMENTAL→COMMERCIAL]** Zero-Zero-Zero by Anrealage; 19 Cracks by Neriage; A/W14 "Transformable" collections.
- **Inflatable / Pneumatic** — air chambers shape the garment. **[EXPERIMENTAL]** RAF Simons for Jil Sander (air-cushioned soles, 2007); ongoing research in soft robotics wearables.

### 1.3 Silhouette Selection Logic

When generating concepts, choose silhouette by answering:

1. **What does the body need to do?** Movement range dictates volume placement.
2. **Where does the garment add value?** Structure where function lives (shoulders for load, waist for fit), volume where expression lives.
3. **What is the garment's primary read?** Power (column), ease (oversized), ceremony (ball gown), utility (cargo), fluidity (bias cut).
4. **Construction feasibility.** A-line is cheaper than bias-cut column; pleats are cheaper than molded panels.

## 2. Garment Construction Fundamentals

### 2.1 Pattern Anatomy

Every garment pattern is a 2D net that is sewn, knitted, or bonded into 3D form. Core pattern pieces:

- **Front/back bodice or body panels** — the torso shell.
- **Sleeves** — set-in (sewn into armhole), raglan (diagonal seam from collar to underarm), kimono (integrated with body), cap sleeve, sleeveless.
- **Skirt/pants panels** — front, back, side, and often yoke or waistband pieces.
- **Facings** — inner layers that finish necklines, armholes, waistbands, front openings. Prevents rolling, adds structure.
- **Linings** — full or partial interior layer for opacity, comfort, drape.
- **Interfacing** — non-visible stabilizer (woven, knit, or nonwoven like Pellon/Fusible) fused or sewn to facings/collars/cuffs.
- **Trim** — elastic, binding, piping, lace, hardware.

### 2.2 Core Construction Techniques

| Technique | Purpose | When used |
|---|---|---|
| **Dart** | Takes in excess fabric to shape 3D body from 2D cloth | Bodices, skirts, tailored jackets. Bust, waist, shoulder, elbow darts |
| **Gusset** | Triangular or diamond insert adds 3D volume at junctions | Underarms, crotch, armpit, between-leg. Baseball glove pattern, historic kimono gussets |
| **Pleat** | Folded fabric creates controlled fullness | Knife, box, accordion, inverted, cartridge. Skirts, dresses, trousers |
| **Tuck** | Narrow stitched fold, subtler than pleat | Blouses, dresses, historical garments |
| **Seam** | Joins two pieces | Plain, French, flat-felled, bound, welt, overlocked/serged |
| **Grading** | Scaling pattern up/down across sizes | Size 8 → 16 typically +2" chest, +1.5" waist, +2" hip per 2 sizes |
| **Draping** | Shaping fabric directly on dress form | Couture, bias-cut, experimental silhouettes |
| **Moulage** | 3D modeling in fabric on the body | Designer's first step; translates to flat pattern |
| **Zero-waste patterning** | Pattern pieces tessellate fabric with minimal offcuts | Contemporary sustainability practice, e.g., Zero Waste Daniel |
| **Pattern cutting vs. 3D knitting vs. whole-garment knitting** | Cut-and-sew; knitted panels then linked; knit in one piece | Sportswear, seamless garments (e.g., Ministry of Supply, Nike's knitted footwear-adjacent uppers) |

### 2.3 Seam Types — Engineering Decisions

- **Plain seam** — two fabric edges right sides together, sewn, pressed open. Default.
- **French seam** — enclosed seam for sheer fabrics; no raw edge exposed.
- **Flat-felled** — one edge folded over the other and topstitched (jeans, shirts). Durable, flat.
- **Welt seam** — fabric strip inserted over a slit; tailored look (pockets, vents).
- **Bound seam** — bias tape encloses raw edge; used on unlined garments, Hong Kong finish.
- **Overlock/serged** — thread-wrapped raw edge; prevents fraying on knits and wovens.
- **Bonded/welded seam** — thermoplastic films or ultrasonic welding replace stitches; seamless look, used in technical outerwear (Gore-Tex laminates).

### 2.4 Grading Logic

Grading is not proportional scaling — it is engineered. Rules of thumb **[VERIFIED]**:

- **Shoulder width** changes less than bust (bodies don't widen linearly).
- **Length** (body, sleeve) changes slowly; **circumference** changes fastest.
- **Ease** is added at pattern stage, then maintained across sizes.
- **Juniors vs. misses vs. plus** use different grade rules — plus sizes need different dart placement, not just larger darts.

### 2.5 Fit Architecture

- **Negative ease** — garment smaller than body (knitwear, swimwear, bodysuits).
- **Zero ease** — garment equals body measurement (fitted wovens).
- **Positive ease** — garment larger than body (shirts, outerwear, dresses).
- **Drape vs. structure** — fluid fabrics (silk crepe, jersey) rely on gravity; structured fabrics (wool suiting, denim) rely on interfacing and seam lines.

## 3. Category-Specific Design Conventions

### 3.1 Tops

- **Shirt**: collar (point, button-down, mandarin/stand, spread, cutaway), placket (button, zip, frog, hidden magnetic), shoulder construction (set-in sleeve, drop shoulder, raglan, kimono), hem (straight, curved, curved-back-longer).
- **T-shirt**: crew/scoop/V-neck; shoulder seam position; sleeve length (sleeveless, cap, short, 3/4, long); body length; hem finishing (coverstitch, twin-needle, raw-edge).
- **Blouse**: more decorative — pintucks, smocking, lace insertion, bishop sleeves.
- **Knit tops**: gauge (needles/inch), stitch structure (jersey, rib, interlock, cable, intarsia), recovery.

### 3.2 Bottoms

- **Trousers**: rise (low, mid, high), leg shape (skinny, straight, tapered, wide, palazzo, harem, cargo), pocket types (slash, welt, patch, cargo flap, hidden), fly construction.
- **Jeans**: denim weight (oz), stretch content, whiskering, distressing (laser, hand-sanded, enzymatic), rivets/bar-tacks at stress points.
- **Skirts**: silhouette (pencil, A-line, circle, pleated, wrap, asymmetric), length, waistband (contour, straight, elastic, paperbag).
- **Shorts**: inseam length, fly, pockets, hem finish.

### 3.3 Dresses

- **By structure**: sheath, shift, fit-and-flare, A-line, mermaid, ball gown, wrap, shirt dress, sweater dress, slip dress.
- **By neckline**: crew, scoop, V, sweetheart, square, off-shoulder, halter, high-neck, cowl.
- **By back**: open, halter, keyhole, illusion, criss-cross, low-cut, covered.
- **Construction details**: bodice/skirt separation, princess seams, waist seam vs. waist-free, lining requirements.

### 3.4 Outerwear

- **Jackets**: blazer (structured shoulder, canvas interlining, lapel), bomber (ribbed cuffs/hem, zip), moto (asymmetric zip, quilted), denim (trucker), puffer (quilted down/synthetic), parka (hood, drawcord), trench (storm flap, epaulettes, D-rings), anorak (pullover, kangaroo pocket), blazer variations.
- **Coats**: overcoat (single/double-breasted, notch/lapel), peacoat, duffle, duster, cape/cloak, poncho, capelet.
- **Vests**: quilted, down, tailored, utility.
- **Key outerwear systems**: layering (base/mid/shell), weatherproofing (DWR finish, membrane, taped seams), packability.

### 3.5 Activewear & Performance Apparel

- **Base layer**: moisture-wicking (polyester, merino, bamboo blends), compression fit, flatlock seams.
- **Mid layer**: insulation (fleece, down, synthetic fill), breathability.
- **Shell**: wind/water-resistant or waterproof-breathable (Gore-Tex, eVent, Pertex, Schoeller).
- **Athletic specifics**: muscle support zones, ventilation mesh placement, reflective elements, chafe-free seams, anti-odor treatments (silver, polygiene).

### 3.6 Couture & Eveningwear

- **Haute couture requirements**: hand-finished seams, custom fit on live body (3+ fittings), hand-sewn beadwork/embroidery, luxury fabrics (silk, satin, organza, lace, tulle, velvet).
- **Eveningwear types**: ball gown, sheath, column, mermaid, pantsuit, jumpsuit, cape ensemble, pantsuit.
- **Bridal**: silhouette, neckline, train length, veil, underskirt (crinoline, horsehair), bustle.

### 3.7 Streetwear & Contemporary Categories

- **Hoodies**: fleece weight, kangaroo vs. set-in pocket, drawcord type, raw hem, printed vs. woven label.
- **Cargo**: pocket count/placement, modular attachments (MOLLE/PALS webbing), utility details.
- **Denim**: rigid vs. stretch, wash (raw, stone, acid, ozone, laser), fit (skinny, straight, loose, wide, baggy).
- **Sneaker-adjacent apparel**: matching sets, vintage-inspired cuts.

## 4. Color Theory for Fashion

### 4.1 Color Terminology

- **Hue** — the color family (red, blue, yellow).
- **Value** — lightness/darkness.
- **Chroma/Saturation** — intensity.
- **Undertone** — warm (yellow-based) vs. cool (blue-based) vs. neutral.
- **Value contrast** — light-on-dark, dark-on-light, tonal (same value).
- **Chroma contrast** — vivid vs. muted.
- **Temperature** — warm colors (reds, oranges, yellows) advance; cool colors (blues, greens, purples) recede.

### 4.2 Color Harmonies

| Scheme | Rule | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Monochromatic** | Same hue, varying value/chroma | Navy + powder blue + sky blue | Cohesive, elongating |
| **Analogous** | Adjacent hues on wheel | Blue, blue-green, green | Harmonious, natural |
| **Complementary** | Opposite hues | Red + green, blue + orange | High contrast, energetic |
| **Split-complementary** | Base + two adjacent to complement | Blue + coral + yellow-orange | Dynamic but balanced |
| **Triadic** | Three equidistant hues | Red + yellow + blue | Vibrant, playful |
| **Tetradic (double complementary)** | Two complementary pairs | Red/green + blue/orange | Rich, complex |
| **Neutral palette** | Black, white, grey, beige, navy | Minimalist wardrobe | Timeless, versatile |

### 4.3 Color in Design Decisions

- **Color placement** creates optical effects: dark colors minimize, light colors emphasize; vertical color blocking elongates; horizontal bands shorten.
- **Color sequencing** in a collection — monochrome story, tonal progression, contrast chapters.
- **Dye methods affect color**: piece-dyed (solid), yarn-dyed (multicolor knit), printed (surface pattern), tie-dye (resist patterns), ombre (gradient), space dye (random blotches).
- **Colorfastness**: washfastness, lightfastness, rubfastness — critical for quality.
- **Pantone Color of the Year** influences commercial color adoption but is not binding.

## 5. Proportion & Balance Principles

### 5.1 Proportion

Proportion is the relationship between parts — not just within a garment but between garment and body.

- **Golden ratio (1:1.618)** appears in classical proportion but is rarely literal in fashion; it's a guideline for pleasing division.
- **Rule of thirds** — placing visual breaks (waist seam, belt, color change) at one-third or two-thirds points creates more interesting proportion than at center.
- **Vertical vs. horizontal emphasis**:
  - Vertical lines (longline coats, column dresses, front seams) elongate.
  - Horizontal lines (belts, color blocks, wide collars) shorten/widen.
  - Diagonal lines create dynamism but can be destabilizing.
- **Scale** — oversized on small frames can overwhelm; petite on tall frames can look undersized. Proportion must serve the wearer.

### 5.2 Balance

Balance is visual weight distribution.

- **Symmetrical balance** — mirror-image left/right. Formal, stable, predictable (most tailoring).
- **Asymmetrical balance** — different elements on each side achieve equal visual weight. Dynamic, modern (one-shoulder dresses, asymmetric hems).
- **Radial balance** — elements radiate from center (circular skirts, circular prints).

### 5.3 Visual Weight Factors

- Dark colors heavier than light.
- Texture (bouclé, tweed, sequins) heavier than smooth (satin, jersey).
- Large prints/patterns heavier than small or solid.
- Embellishment concentrates weight.
- Structured fabrics heavier than fluid.

### 5.4 Body Proportion Interaction

Designers manipulate perceived body proportion:

- **Longer torso illusion**: high-rise bottoms, tucked tops, vertical details at waist.
- **Shorter torso illusion**: low-rise, long tops, horizontal waist emphasis.
- **Longer legs illusion**: high-rise + cropped top, vertical seam placement, pointed shoes.
- **Shorter legs illusion**: mid-rise, longer tops, horizontal hem details.

## 6. Design Vocabulary Glossary

### 6.1 Garment Terms

- **A-line**: flares from fitted top in straight line to hem.
- **Banana hem**: curved hem, higher at sides, lower at center (or vice versa).
- **Basque waist**: fitted waist seam with slight point at front.
- **Bateau / boat neck**: wide, straight-across neckline following collarbone.
- **Bias cut**: fabric cut at 45° to grain; maximum drape and cling.
- **Blouson**: gathered or elasticized waist creating bloused effect.
- **Bodice**: upper part of dress or bodysuit.
- **Bodysuit**: one-piece garment covering torso and crotch, often with snaps at inseam.
- **Bustier**: strapless, structured, often boned, fashion or foundation garment.
- **Cap sleeve**: very short sleeve ending at shoulder point.
- **Cowl neck**: draped, loose neckline falling in folds.
- **Crew neck**: round neckline sitting at base of neck.
- **Dart**: tapered fold stitched to shape fabric.
- **Drop waist**: waist seam positioned below natural waist (1930s style) or at hip.
- **Empire waist**: seam just below bust.
- **Fit-and-flare**: fitted bodice, flared skirt.
- **Godet**: triangular fabric insert adding flare (skirts, sleeves).
- **Gusset**: diamond/triangular insert at junction points.
- **Halter**: neckline where fabric ties/attaches behind neck, exposing shoulders and upper back.
- **Kimono sleeve**: sleeve cut in one piece with body, wide at shoulder.
- **Leg-of-mutton sleeve**: full at upper arm, tapered to wrist (historical).
- **Low-rise**: waistband sitting below natural waist (early 2000s).
- **Mandarin collar**: short, stand-up collar.
- **Mermaid**: fitted through thigh, flares at/below knee.
- **Mullet hem**: short in front, long in back.
- **Off-shoulder**: neckline sits below shoulder, exposing shoulders.
- **Peplum**: short flared ruffle at waist.
- **Pintuck**: narrow, closely spaced tucks for texture.
- **Poncho**: one-piece, head opening, no sleeves.
- **Princess seam**: vertical seam from shoulder/bust to hem, no waist dart.
- **Raglan sleeve**: diagonal seam from neckline to underarm.
- **Set-in sleeve**: sleeve sewn into armhole with shoulder seam.
- **Shawl collar**: wide, notched-less collar lying flat, often velvet.
- **Sheath**: narrow, straight, body-skimming dress.
- **Shift**: straight, unfitted dress hanging from shoulders.
- **Skater skirt**: short, flared circle skirt.
- **Slit**: opening in skirt or dress (center, side, back).
- **Smocking**: gathered fabric controlled by decorative stitches.
- **Surplice / wrap**: overlapping front panels.
- **Sweetheart**: neckline curving like top of heart.
- **Tailored**: structured, fitted, professional (vs. casual).
- **Turtleneck**: high, close-fitting neckline covering neck.
- **Welt**: raised strip over opening (pocket, seam).
- **Wrap dress**: diagonal overlapping bodice and skirt.
- **Yoke**: fitted section at shoulder/bust with skirt attached below.

### 6.2 Fabric & Texture Terms

- **Basket weave**: weave pattern with groups of threads crossing (canvas, monk's cloth).
- **Bouclé**: nubby, looped yarn texture (sweaters, jackets).
- **Challis**: lightweight woven, often printed (Rayon challis blouses).
- **Charmeuse**: satin weave with matte back, lustrous front (silk, polyester).
- **Chiffon**: sheer, lightweight, plain or twill weave (silk, polyester).
- **Corduroy**: cut-pile fabric with vertical ribs (wale).
- **Crepe**: pebbled texture, dull surface, good drape (silk, wool, polyester crepe).
- **Denim**: twill weave cotton, usually blue, durable.
- **Dobby**: small geometric woven patterns.
- **Faille**: ribbed, crisp, medium-weight (silk, rayon).
- **Flannel**: brushed or napped wool/cotton, soft.
- **Fleece**: knitted, brushed pile, warm.
- **Gabardine**: tightly woven twill, durable, weather-resistant (wool suits).
- **Gingham**: checked pattern, woven.
- **Jacquard**: complex woven patterns (damask, brocade, matelassé).
- **Jersey**: knit fabric, single knit, stretchy, soft.
- **Lace**: openwork fabric, decorative (Chantilly, Alençon, guipure, crochet).
- **Lawn**: fine, crisp cotton, semi-sheer.
- **Matelassé**: quilted, padded jacquard pattern.
- **Muslin**: plain weave cotton, used for toiles/mocks.
- **Organza**: crisp, sheer, lightweight (silk, polyester).
- **Ottoman**: ribbed, slightly stretchy fabric.
- **Piqué**: textured knit or weave with raised pattern (polo shirts).
- **Poplin**: crisp, plain weave, slight rib.
- **Sateen**: satin weave with cotton feel, matte sheen.
- **Seersucker**: puckered striped fabric, cool.
- **Shantung**: slubbed silk, textured.
- **Taffeta**: crisp, smooth, rustling (silk, polyester).
- **Tencel/Lyocell**: regenerated cellulose, soft, breathable.
- **Tweed**: rough, textured woolen fabric.
- **Twill**: diagonal weave pattern (denim, chino).
- **Velour**: knit pile fabric, soft, velvety.
- **Velvet**: cut-pile fabric, luxurious sheen.
- **Voile**: light, semi-sheer, crisp.
- **Worsted**: smooth, tightly spun wool yarn.

### 6.3 Construction & Production Terms

- **Armhole**: opening for arm/sleeve.
- **Bias**: 45° angle to fabric grain.
- **Basting**: temporary long stitches for fitting.
- **Block**: master pattern used for grading.
- **Bobbin**: small spool for lower thread in lockstitch machine.
- **Bolt**: large roll of fabric.
- **Casing**: folded channel for elastic/cord.
- **Clipping**: cutting into seam allowance to allow curve to lie flat.
- **Cutting**: pattern layout on fabric (marker), then cutting pieces.
- **Ease**: extra room in garment beyond body measurement.
- **Facing**: inner layer finishing edges.
- **Grainline**: direction of warp threads; pattern must align.
- **Hemming**: finishing raw edge at garment bottom.
- **Interfacing**: stabilizer layer.
- **Lining**: inner layer for opacity/comfort.
- **Marker**: layout of pattern pieces on fabric for cutting.
- **Notch**: small mark on pattern/seam for alignment.
- **Pattern**: paper template for cutting fabric pieces.
- **Pressing**: using iron to shape seams/fabric (not ironing, which flattens).
- **Seam allowance**: extra fabric beyond stitch line (typically 1/4"–5/8").
- **Selvage/selvedge**: self-finished edge of fabric.
- **Staystitching**: stitching to prevent stretching (necklines, armholes).
- **Toile/muslin**: test garment in cheap fabric.
- **Understitching**: stitching facing to seam allowance to prevent rolling.
- **Warp**: lengthwise yarns in weaving.
- **Weft**: crosswise yarns.

## QUICK REFERENCE

**Silhouette decision tree:**
- Need structure/support? → Column, sheath, tailored
- Need movement/volume? → A-line, oversized, wide-leg
- Need drama/ceremony? → Ball gown, mermaid, architectural
- Need utility/cargo? → Deconstructed, modular, utility
- Need fluidity/drape? → Bias cut, slip, bias column

**Construction sequence:**
1. Concept + silhouette
2. Moulage or flat pattern
3. Muslin/toile fitting
4. Sample in target fabric

**Color selection:**
- Monochromatic = safe, elongating
- Analogous = harmonious, natural
- Complementary = bold, high contrast
- Neutral = timeless, versatile
- Consider undertone matching to skin

**Proportion tools:**
- Vertical lines = elongate
- Horizontal lines = shorten/widen
- Rule of thirds = interesting division
- Dark colors = minimize
- Light colors = emphasize

**Glossary lookup:** Use section 6 for any unfamiliar term. Most terms are industry-standard and can be searched online for images.
